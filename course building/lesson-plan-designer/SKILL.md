# Lesson Plan Designer

Builds a single class session plan, minute-by-minute, from topic and context. Follow this sequence exactly.

---

## Step 1: Read context first

Before asking any questions, read the user's ABOUT ME folder or project context if available. Look for:
- Their discipline, institution, and teaching level
- Their stated pedagogical approach (mastery-based, traditional, mixed)
- Any prior lesson plans or activity preferences noted
- Class size norms, typical modality, or tech constraints

Use what you find to avoid redundant questions and to inform tone, complexity, and activity design.

---

## Step 2: Gather inputs

Ask all six questions in a single grouped message. Do not send them one at a time.

```
To build your lesson plan, I need six things:

1. **Course and session topic** — What course is this for, and what is this specific
   session about? A sentence or two on what the session covers is enough.

2. **Session length and class size** — How many minutes is the session? How many
   students will be there? (Approximate is fine.)

3. **Modality** — How is the class meeting?
   - In-person
   - Online synchronous (Zoom, Teams, etc.)
   - Hybrid (some in-person, some remote simultaneously)

4. **The one thing students must leave with** — If everything else falls apart, what
   is the single concept, skill, or insight students cannot leave without? Be specific.
   "Understanding X" is too broad — try "Students can distinguish X from Y" or
   "Students can apply X to a new problem."

5. **What they were supposed to do before class** — What's the pre-class expectation?
   (Assigned reading, video, problem set, nothing, or something else.) Be honest if
   they probably won't have done it — I'll plan for that.

6. **Materials and tech available** — What can you use in this session? Check all
   that apply or just describe what's available:
   - Projector / screen
   - Whiteboard or chalkboard
   - Student laptops or phones
   - Poll or response tools (Poll Everywhere, iClicker, Mentimeter, etc.)
   - Breakout rooms (if online)
   - Anything else worth knowing
```

Wait for the instructor's full response before proceeding.

---

## Step 3: Generate the lesson plan

Produce all eight sections in a single response. The plan must be usable as a physical or digital reference during class — concrete, timed, and ready to run.

---

### Section 1: Session at a Glance

A compact header block the instructor can print or pin:

```
Course:          [course name]
Topic:           [session topic]
Date/Time:       [leave blank for instructor to fill]
Length:          [X minutes]
Class size:      [N students]
Modality:        [in-person / online sync / hybrid]
Must-leave-with: [the one thing from input 4]
Pre-class:       [what was assigned]
```

---

### Section 2: Opening Hook (3–5 minutes)

Design a hook that **creates a question**, not just introduces a topic. The hook should make students feel the tension or problem the session will resolve. Do not use "Today we'll be covering..." as an opening.

Options that work well: a surprising fact, a short case or scenario, a provocative claim, a short video clip cue, a problem they can't yet solve, or a question with no obvious right answer.

Provide:
- **The hook itself** — written out as the instructor would deliver or display it
- **The question it creates** — the intellectual itch that the session will scratch
- **Time:** 3–5 minutes

---

### Section 3: Minute-by-Minute Plan

Build the full session timeline from 0:00 to end. Format as a table:

| Time | Duration | Activity | Purpose | Notes |
|------|----------|----------|---------|-------|
| 0:00 | 5 min | Opening hook | Create the question | See Section 2 |
| ... | ... | ... | ... | ... |

Rules for the plan:
- Every block must have a clear **purpose** — what it does for learning, not just what happens
- No lecture block longer than 15 minutes without a break or interaction
- Include at least 2 active learning moments (see Section 4)
- Write transitions explicitly as their own rows (see Section 5)
- Include the formative check at or near the halfway point (see Section 6)
- Reserve the final 5–8 minutes for the closing reflection (see Section 7)
- For hybrid sessions, flag which activities need special facilitation for remote students

If the session is short (≤50 minutes), every minute matters — say so if the plan is tight and identify where to cut first.

---

### Section 4: Active Learning Moments (minimum 2)

Pull out and describe each active learning moment from the plan above. For each:
- **Activity name** — a short label (Think-Pair-Share, Muddiest Point, Gallery Walk, Case Analysis, Jigsaw, etc.)
- **What students do** — described from the student's perspective, not the instructor's
- **Why it's here** — what this specific activity does at this point in the session
- **Modality notes** — if online or hybrid, explain exactly how this works (breakout rooms, chat waterfall, shared doc, etc.)
- **Timing** — pulled from the plan

Do not use the same activity format twice unless the session clearly calls for it.

---

### Section 5: Transitions Written Out

List every transition in the session as it would be spoken or displayed. Transitions are the connective tissue that keep sessions from feeling like a series of disconnected activities.

Format:
> **Transition [N]** *(from [Activity A] to [Activity B])*
> "[Exact words or slide text the instructor uses]"

Each transition should:
- Briefly name what just happened
- Connect it to what's coming next
- Take no more than 60 seconds

---

### Section 6: Formative Check at the Midpoint

Design one formative check to run at or near the session's halfway mark. Its purpose is to reveal where students are before the second half of class — so the instructor can adjust if needed.

Provide:
- **The check** — exactly what the instructor asks or displays
- **Format** — how students respond (show of hands, poll, chat, exit card, pair discussion, quick write, etc.)
- **What to listen for** — 2–3 signals that tell the instructor the class is ready to move forward
- **What to do if they're not** — one specific recovery move (not "re-teach everything")
- **Time:** 3–5 minutes

Match the format to the modality. A chat waterfall works online; a hand signal works in-person.

---

### Section 7: Closing Reflection

Design a closing that does two things: consolidates what students just learned, and points explicitly toward the next session.

Provide:
- **The prompt** — what students respond to (written, spoken, or digital)
- **The bridge** — one or two sentences the instructor says that connect this session to what comes next
- **Format** — how students respond and whether it's collected
- **Time:** 4–6 minutes

Avoid closings that just summarize the lecture. The closing should require students to do something with what they learned — apply, connect, or question.

---

### Section 8: Backup Move

Describe one "energy drop" intervention the instructor can deploy if the class loses momentum, goes quiet, or the main activity falls flat.

Provide:
- **The move** — a concrete, low-prep alternative activity
- **When to use it** — the signal that it's time to shift
- **How to pivot** — the exact words to transition into it without making the class feel like things went wrong
- **Time cost** — how many minutes it takes

The backup move should be executable with no extra materials and no explanation of why the plan changed.

---

### Section 9: Materials List

A flat checklist of everything the instructor needs to run this session:

- [ ] [Item] — [brief note if needed, e.g., "print 1 per student" or "share link before class"]

Organize by: **Before class** / **During class** / **Tech/tools**. Flag anything that requires setup time or testing before students arrive.

---

## Step 4: Offer next steps

After delivering the plan, offer these briefly:

1. **Adapt** — adjust for a shorter/longer session, different modality, or lower/higher energy class
2. **Add assessment** — build a quick quiz, exit ticket, or rubric that maps to the session's must-leave-with
3. **Link to objectives** — connect this plan to formally written learning objectives (invokes the Learning Objectives Writer skill)
